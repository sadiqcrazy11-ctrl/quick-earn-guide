About this tool

What this loan calculator does

A loan's monthly payment hides its real price. This loan calculator shows the payment, the total interest, and a year-by-year amortization schedule — how much of each year's payments went to interest and how much actually reduced the balance. Then it runs the same loan with an extra monthly payment, so the saving from paying a little more is a concrete number instead of a vague promise.

A quick example

With the defaults — $25,000 at 8% over 5 years — the monthly payment is $506.91 and total interest is about $5,415, so the loan costs about $30,415 all in. Add $100 extra a month and the loan ends in 49 months instead of 60, with total interest of about $4,325 — saving roughly $1,089 and 11 months. The extra $100 is the same money either way; paying it early is what buys the saving.

How to use it — 3 steps

  1. Enter the loan amount, the APR from the offer, and the term in years.
  2. Add any extra amount you could pay every month, even a small one.
  3. Compare the two payoff times and interest totals, and check the schedule to see how slowly the balance falls in year one.

Who it is for

Anyone weighing a personal loan, car loan or student loan offer, and anyone already in a loan wondering whether extra payments are worth it. It works for any fixed-rate installment loan with monthly payments.

Honest limitations

The rate is held fixed; variable-rate loans will differ. Origination fees, gap insurance, late fees and prepayment penalties are excluded, and the schedule assumes every payment lands on time. If your loan compounds differently (some auto loans use daily interest), the figures will be close but not exact. Estimates only — not financial advice.

FAQ

Loan Calculator — common questions

How does the loan calculator work?

It uses the standard amortization formula: the monthly payment is the loan amount times the monthly rate, divided by one minus the monthly rate factor over the full term. It then simulates month by month, charging interest on the remaining balance and applying the rest of each payment to principal, which is how the year-by-year schedule is built.

Why is the first year mostly interest?

Because interest is charged on the full balance, and the balance is highest at the start. Early payments therefore cover a large interest charge and only a small principal slice. As the balance falls, the interest charge shrinks and more of each fixed payment reaches principal — the schedule shows this shift year by year.

Do extra payments really save that much?

Yes, because every extra dollar goes to principal (if your lender applies it that way), which lowers every future interest charge. On the default loan, $100 extra a month saves about $1,089 of interest and 11 months. The earlier the extra payments start, the larger the saving.

What is the difference between APR and interest rate?

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the principal. APR adds certain fees into an annualized rate, so it is usually slightly higher and is the fairer number for comparing offers. This calculator uses the single rate you enter for the whole schedule, so entering the APR gives the more honest comparison.

About this tool

What this credit card payoff calculator does

Minimum payments are designed to feel affordable and last a very long time: as the balance falls, the minimum falls with it, so the payment shrinks every month and the debt lingers for years. This credit card payoff calculator simulates that trap on one card — minimum-only payments month by month — against a fixed payment that stays the same until the card is clear, and shows the payoff time, total interest and debt-free date for both.

A quick example

With the defaults — a $5,000 balance at 24% APR, minimums of 1% of the balance plus interest with a $25 floor — minimum-only payments take 234 months (19 years and 6 months) and cost about $8,887 in interest, more than the original balance. A fixed $150 a month clears the same card in 56 months with about $3,322 of interest. The fixed payment saves 178 months and about $5,565. The first minimum payment is $150.00 either way — the difference is refusing to let it shrink.

How to use it — 3 steps

  1. Enter the card balance and APR from your statement.
  2. Set the minimum-payment rule: the percentage, plus the dollar floor your issuer uses.
  3. Enter a fixed payment you can sustain, then compare the two payoff times and interest totals.

Who it is for

Anyone carrying one card balance and paying the minimum, and anyone deciding between a balance transfer, a fixed payment plan, or minimums. For several debts at once with snowball-versus-avalanche ordering, use the debt payoff calculator.

Honest limitations

Issuer minimum formulas vary — some use 2% or 3% of the balance, some use interest plus a flat amount — so check your cardholder agreement and adjust the percentage and floor to match. Penalty APRs, fees, promotional rates and new charges are excluded. If the fixed payment is below the monthly interest charge, the debt never shrinks, and the calculator will say so. FAQ

Credit Card Payoff Calculator — common questions

How does the credit card payoff calculator work?

Minimum-only mode simulates month by month: interest accrues on the balance, the minimum is the percentage of the balance plus that month's interest (or the dollar floor, whichever is higher), and the balance falls by the payment minus the interest. Fixed-payment mode pays the same amount every month until the balance reaches zero. Both simulations count months and total interest.

Why do minimum payments take so long?

Because the minimum shrinks as the balance shrinks. Early on, most of each minimum covers interest and only a small slice reduces principal. As the balance falls slowly, the minimum falls too, so the principal slice stays small for years. A fixed payment breaks the cycle: as interest falls, more of every payment reaches principal.

What if my payment does not cover the interest?

Then the balance grows every month even though you are paying. This is negative amortization, and no payoff date exists until the payment rises above the monthly interest charge. The calculator flags this case. The fixes are a larger payment, a lower rate through a balance transfer or hardship program, or help from a nonprofit credit counselor.

Is a fixed payment always better than the minimum?

For total cost and payoff speed, yes — as long as it is affordable every month. The risk of a fixed payment is committing to more than the budget allows and missing a payment, which brings fees and penalty rates. Pick the highest fixed payment you can sustain without fail, and automate it.

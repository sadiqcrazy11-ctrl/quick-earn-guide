About this tool

What this home affordability calculator does

Lenders judge affordability with two ratios, and the stricter one wins. The front-end ratio caps housing costs — principal, interest, tax, insurance and HOA — at 28% of gross monthly income. The back-end ratio caps all monthly debts including housing at 36%. This home affordability calculator applies both, takes the lower housing budget, and solves backwards through the mortgage formula to the maximum home price, counting property tax on the price itself rather than pretending tax is fixed.

A quick example

With the defaults — $90,000 of income, $500 of other monthly debts, $40,000 down, 6.5% over 30 years — the front-end rule allows $2,100 a month for housing and the back-end rule allows $2,200, so the front-end rule binds. The maximum home price is about $300,905, with a loan of about $260,905. Raise other debts to $1,500 and the back-end rule binds instead at $1,200 a month, cutting the maximum price to about $177,965. Debts move the ceiling fast.

How to use it — 3 steps

  1. Enter gross annual income before tax, other monthly debt payments, and your down payment.
  2. Add the rate, term, property tax rate, insurance and HOA dues for the area you are shopping in.
  3. Read the maximum price and which limit binds — then change debts or down payment to see what raises the ceiling.

Who it is for

Buyers setting a realistic price range before falling for a listing, and anyone deciding whether paying down debt or saving a bigger down payment helps affordability more. It is a planning ceiling, not a pre-approval.

Honest limitations

The 28/36 rule is a guideline: FHA, VA, USDA and conventional programs each have their own DTI standards, and automated underwriting can approve up to 50% back-end DTI with compensating factors. Credit score, down payment source, reserves and employment history all matter to a lender and none are modeled here. Property tax is estimated as a flat percentage of price. FAQ

Home Affordability Calculator — common questions

How does the home affordability calculator work?

It converts annual income to gross monthly income, applies the front-end limit (28% by default) and the back-end limit (36% minus other debts) to get two housing budgets, and takes the lower one. It then solves the mortgage formula in reverse: the loan that fits the budget after property tax, insurance and HOA are counted, plus the down payment, is the maximum price.

What is the 28/36 rule?

A lending guideline: monthly housing costs should not exceed 28% of gross monthly income (the front-end ratio), and total monthly debt payments including housing should not exceed 36% (the back-end ratio). Both are measured against gross income, before tax. It is a rule of thumb used by many lenders, not a law, and programs differ.

Why does the back-end limit sometimes bind instead?

Because other debts eat the same 36% budget. With no other debts, the front-end rule usually binds. Add a car payment, student loans and card minimums, and the back-end budget — 36% of income minus those debts — can fall below the front-end budget. The calculator names the binding limit so you know which lever to pull.

Is the maximum price what I should spend?

No — it is a ceiling, not a target. The rule uses gross income and ignores groceries, utilities, transport, childcare, savings and everything else life costs. Many buyers are comfortable well below the maximum. Treat the figure as the top of the range and shop with room to breathe underneath it.

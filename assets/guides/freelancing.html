How to Start Freelancing: Quick-Start on Fiverr & Upwork

By Sadiq Khan · Published October 2026 · 12 min read · Updated on November 21, 2026

Share:WhatsAppTelegramX

Quick Answer: Freelancing is usually the fastest legitimate way to earn your first dollar online, because you sell a skill directly to someone who needs it today — no audience, no thresholds, no waiting for an algorithm. Pick one specific service, build three samples, price low on purpose to earn your first reviews, and find clients both on Fiverr and Upwork and by searching Google for public LinkedIn profiles of decision-makers in one city. A beginner gig priced at $15–$40 that earns five 5-star reviews is worth more than a $150 gig nobody buys.

Writing, design, video editing, spreadsheets, virtual assistance, translation, and basic web work all sell every day on Fiverr and Upwork. The work is real, the first dollar can come fast, and the skill you build — delivering for a paying client — transfers to every other way of earning online. This guide covers the profile, the pricing, the first reviews, and a free way to find clients outside the platforms.

Set up a profile that gets hired

  1. Pick one specific service. "I design logos" beats "I do everything." Buyers search for specifics.
  2. Use a clear photo and a plain-English headline that names the result: "I will edit your YouTube videos with captions and hooks," not "Video Expert Pro."
  3. Show samples, even self-made ones. No clients yet? Create 3 sample projects as if they were real jobs. Buyers judge what they can see.
  4. Write your description around the buyer's problem — what they get, how fast, and what you need from them to start.

The most common beginner mistake is listing five services at once. A buyer looking for a thumbnail designer will not hire a profile that also offers data entry, translation, and logo design. One service, one headline, three samples — that is a profile a buyer can trust in ten seconds.

Pricing and your first month

Price low at the start — on purpose. Your first goal is not profit; it is reviews. A beginner gig priced at, for example, $15–$40 that earns five 5-star reviews is worth more than a $150 gig nobody buys. That $15–$40 figure is an example starting price, not a market-wide beginner rate — scope, revisions, turnaround, client budget, and platform fees all affect your final take-home pay. First-month earnings can range from $0 to whatever clients agree to pay for completed work: some beginners secure a small project quickly, while others spend weeks building samples and pitching without a paid order. Results depend on skill, portfolio quality, competition, proposal volume, pricing, and client demand. Treat any dollar figure — here or anywhere else — as an example, not a typical or guaranteed result. After 10–20 good reviews, raise prices gradually.

Know the platform's cut before you price. Fiverr takes a flat 20% from sellers on every completed order, including extras and tips — so a $50 order pays you $40 before taxes. Upwork's fee is different: a flat 10% on contracts formed before May 2025, and a variable 0–15% per contract on contracts formed on or after May 2025, disclosed at inception and locked for that contract. Check the current fee page on each platform before you quote, because fees change and a quote built on last year's fee is a quote that loses money.

A worked example: product descriptions

Say your skill is writing product descriptions. A clear beginner offer might look like this: 5 product descriptions for a $50 project price — up to 150 words each, one revision included, delivered in 3 days. The math on Fiverr, using the platform fee in Fiverr's current official policy (check the Fiverr Help Center for the current fee before you price):

  • Gross amount (what the client pays): $50
  • Platform fee (20% of the order, per Fiverr's current official seller policy): $10
  • Estimated take-home: $40 — before taxes and any other costs

On Upwork the fee structure is different — check the Upwork Help Center for the current platform rules and fees before you quote. For broader context only, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics page for Writers and Authors publishes occupational wage data. That is economy-wide context for the occupation — it is not a freelance-platform beginner rate, and it does not predict what a new Fiverr or Upwork seller will earn.

Set aside roughly 25–30% of earnings for US self-employment taxes as a planning cushion — that percentage is a rule of thumb, not your exact rate, and a tax professional can give you the exact number for your situation. All income is taxable whether or not you receive a form: for the 2026 tax year the IRS 1099-K reporting threshold is more than $20,000 in payments and more than 200 transactions in the calendar year, but that threshold only decides when a platform sends you a form — you owe tax on smaller amounts too. Keep a simple log from day one: date, client, work done, amount paid.

Getting your first reviews

  • On Upwork, send short, specific proposals that mention the client's actual project — never copy-paste blasts.
  • Deliver early, communicate before the client has to ask, and include one small extra (an alternate version, a bonus size).
  • When the job ends well, politely ask for an honest review. Most happy clients are glad to leave one.
  • Never buy fake reviews and never work off-platform to dodge fees — both get accounts banned, and a banned account ends the business.

Reviews are the whole game in month one. Ten genuine 5-star reviews will do more for your next hundred dollars than any course, and they compound: each review makes the next buyer more likely to click, which makes the next review easier to earn. Protect that asset. One rushed job with a 1-star review costs more than the money it paid.

How to find clients with one Google search: the LinkedIn formula

Platforms are not the only place clients live. LinkedIn hides most profiles behind its own search limits and paid plans — but Google has already indexed millions of public LinkedIn profiles. One search line pulls up decision-makers by exact job title and city: the people who can actually hire a freelancer or a small agency. It costs nothing, needs no Sales Navigator, and takes about ten seconds. You are not hacking anything — you are asking Google a sharper question, because a public LinkedIn profile is just a web page, and finding web pages is Google's whole job.

The formula, part by part:

site:linkedin.com/in "vp of sales" "austin, texas"

  • site:linkedin.com/in — tells Google: only individual LinkedIn profile pages. Not articles, not company pages, not job posts.
  • "vp of sales" — the exact job title, in quotes. Quotes force an exact match, so you get sales leaders instead of everyone who ever typed the word sales.
  • "austin, texas" — the city, also in quotes. Swap in any city where your clients are. Title plus city is the whole trick: the right person, in the right place.

Copy a line, change two things. Keep the site: part exactly as it is, and swap the title and the city for your own market.

From search results to a client list

  1. Pick your buyer first. One service, one title, one city. "Video editing for real estate brokers in Miami" beats "anyone, anywhere" every time.
  2. Open profiles one at a time. Note each person's name, title, company, and profile link in a simple spreadsheet. Five columns is plenty.
  3. Find one personal detail per person. A recent post, a new location, a product launch — something their business is doing right now. That detail is what separates your message from spam.
  4. Build the list by hand — 20 to 30 profiles a day. Slow is not a flaw here. A short list of the right people beats a scraped list of thousands, and a human pace keeps your account safe (see the warning below).
  5. Send a short, personal message. Use the template below. If there is no reply, one follow-up after 4–5 days — then move on. No third chase, ever.

A first message that gets replies

Short, specific, and about them — not a pitch deck. Fill in the brackets from the personal detail you found in step 3:

Hi [Name] — noticed [Company] has been [specific detail: posting a lot of short videos lately / just opened a second location]. I help [type of business] with [your service + the result, e.g. turning those posts into enquiries]. I have two quick ideas for your page — want me to send them over? No charge, just the ideas.

Keep it manual — this part matters: Build the list by hand and write each message yourself. LinkedIn restricts accounts that view profiles at bot speed or use scraping and auto-messaging tools, and a restricted account ends your outreach completely. Never buy scraped lead lists, never blast the same copy to hundreds of people, and stop messaging anyone who says no. Twenty to thirty careful profiles a day is a pace you can keep up for months.

Honest expectations: The formula finds people, not clients. Clients come from the personal detail, a relevant offer, and showing up every day. Expect a reply from roughly 1 in 10 good messages, and a first client after about 2–4 weeks of steady outreach. That is normal — and it is still faster than waiting for a platform algorithm to notice you.

Choosing your first service: what sells

If you do not yet know what to sell, start from skills you already have rather than skills you would need months to learn. Writing and editing, short-form video editing, thumbnail design, spreadsheet cleanup, data entry, transcription, captioning, and virtual assistance all sell every day, and all of them can be learned to a sellable standard in weeks, not years. The test is simple: can you produce three samples this week that a stranger would pay for? If yes, that is your service. If no, pick something simpler and build the samples first.

A worked way to choose: list five things you can already do, score each one on (a) can I show three samples this week, (b) do businesses pay for this every week, and (c) can I deliver it in under a week. The service with three yeses wins. Everything else waits until you have reviews.

Common mistakes

  • Listing five services at once. Buyers hire specialists. One service, one headline.
  • Pricing on hope instead of math. A $50 gig is $40 after Fiverr's 20% fee, before tax. Price with the real numbers in mind.
  • Copy-paste proposals. A proposal that mentions the client's actual project beats ten generic blasts.
  • Working off-platform to dodge fees. It gets accounts banned — and you lose the platform's payment protection when a client does not pay.
  • Buying fake reviews or scraped lead lists. Both end accounts. Reviews and lists only work when they are real.
  • Chasing a non-reply. One follow-up after 4–5 days, then move on. A third chase burns the relationship and your time.
  • Waiting for the algorithm. Platforms notice profiles with reviews. Outreach — the LinkedIn formula above, direct pitches to local businesses — is how the first reviews arrive.

Honest bottom line: Freelancing trades time for money at first, and that is fine — it is real, fast, and skill-building. Protect yourself: use the platform's payment system, get the scope in writing, and set aside roughly 25–30% of earnings for US self-employment taxes as a planning cushion.

FAQs

How fast can I get my first freelance client?

Some beginners secure a small project in the first week; others spend weeks building samples and pitching without a paid order. With steady outreach — 20–30 LinkedIn profiles a day, plus platform proposals — a first client after about 2–4 weeks is a normal expectation, not a promise.

Should I start on Fiverr or Upwork?

Both work. Fiverr suits packaged gigs with a fixed price; Upwork suits custom proposals for specific client projects. Many freelancers run both, with the same one service and the same three samples on each.

How much should I charge as a beginner?

Low enough to earn reviews, high enough to survive the platform fee. A $15–$40 example starting price is a common beginner range for small gigs; after 10–20 good reviews, raise prices gradually.

Is the LinkedIn search formula allowed?

Yes — you are searching Google for public profile pages. What gets accounts restricted is bot-speed viewing, scraping, and auto-messaging. Build the list by hand, write each message yourself, and keep the pace at 20–30 profiles a day.

Do I need to pay tax on freelance income?

Yes. All income is taxable whether or not you receive a form. The IRS 1099-K threshold (more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions) only decides when a platform sends you a form. This is general information, not tax advice.

Side Hustle Average Income: $810 a Month in 2026 (Real Data)

By Sadiq Khan · Published November 11, 2026 · 12 min read · Updated on November 21, 2026

Share:WhatsAppTelegramX

Quick Answer: Side hustle average income in 2026 is $810 a month, according to the figure most widely cited as of November 2026 — 19% short of the $1,000 target nearly every side-hustle list promises. The median is far lower: Bankrate's 2025 survey puts it at just $200 a month, meaning half of all side hustlers earn $200 or less. Only 9% earn more than $2,000 a month. The most common hustle is selling goods online, and the cost side is real too: a 2026 poll found 54% of side hustlers say the extra work increased their stress or disrupted their sleep. The gap between the $1,000 promise and the $810 average is the real story — and knowing it is what keeps you from quitting in week three.

Type "$1,000 a month side hustle" into any search box and you will get dozens of lists that treat the number as a starting point. The surveys say something different. The $810 average is the most-cited figure as of November 2026, repeated by Forbes Advisor. Bankrate's 2025 survey puts the average at $885 and the median at $200. LendingTree's March 2026 survey lands higher, at a $1,242 average — but its median is $400. Every source agrees on the shape: a small group of high earners pulls the average up, while the typical side hustler earns a few hundred dollars a month.

The scale is still enormous. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 16.5 million Americans as independent contractors or gig workers in the fourth quarter of 2024 — 10.1% of all employment — and BLS estimates put collective gig earnings above $204 billion in 2024. Divide that across 16.5 million workers and you get roughly $1,030 a month per worker, right in the same neighborhood as the survey figures. About 27% of US adults have a side hustle, with Gen Z leading at 34%.

Why Average and Median Disagree

The gap is not a mistake; it is the whole point. Bankrate's 2025 survey shows the distribution clearly: 25% of side hustlers earn more than $500 a month, 14% earn more than $1,000 a month, and only 9% earn more than $2,000 a month. The biggest single bracket — 28% of side hustlers — earns just $1 to $50 a month. So $1,000 sits roughly at the line between casual participation and a genuine second income, and $2,000 is well past it.

The people earning real money are doing skilled work: freelance writing, tutoring, web design, AI services for small businesses. The people earning $50 a month are mostly doing low-effort work — surveys, microtasks, casual reselling. That split is the most useful fact in this whole guide: the income follows the skill, not the hustle category.

Forbes Advisor adds a warning worth hearing before you buy inventory: inventory-based side hustles fail about 80% of the time in their first year. That is the category behind most "I made $10,000 in my first month" stories, and the failure rate is the part those stories leave out.

What Most People Actually Do: Selling Online

Scroll through money advice online and you would think everyone is a content creator. The surveys tell a different story. Content creation accounts for only about 10 percent of side hustlers, even though 26 percent of Gen Z say they want to become creators. The hustle most Americans actually do is far less glamorous: selling things online.

According to recent survey roundups, 44 to 48 percent of side hustlers earn from ecommerce, ahead of freelance work at 21 to 23 percent and ride-share or delivery at 19 percent. The most popular platforms are Amazon (used by 52 percent), eBay (38 percent), and peer-to-peer marketplaces such as Facebook Marketplace and Poshmark (58 percent of ecommerce hustlers). It is common because it is easy to start, not because it is easy to get rich from.

That finding is worth reading carefully. Different surveys define categories differently, so the exact ranking moves — one survey, for example, puts transportation and delivery and creative services tied at 25 percent each, with ecommerce at 21 percent. The consistent point across them is the same: selling online is always near the top, and it is usually first. It is the default side hustle because almost anyone can list something tonight.

The best-selling categories are not exotic. Handmade or custom goods account for 36 percent of sellers, vintage or second-hand items 35 percent, and print-on-demand products 35 percent. In plain terms, people sell what they make, what they already own, and what they design but never stock.

One large survey found that 62 percent of side hustlers earn up to $500 a month after taxes, while about 20 percent earn more than $1,000. Those are real earnings, but they are side income, not a salary replacement. Most side hustlers also work fewer than 10 hours a week on their hustle, which explains both the modest totals and the popularity: it fits around a job.

Fees and costs eat into the headline price, too. Amazon, eBay, and Etsy all take cuts, shipping costs money, and returns happen. Local cash sales on Facebook Marketplace keep more of the sale price because there is no shipping and no platform fee, which is why furniture, appliances, and baby gear sell best locally. Do the net math on every sale, not just the listing price.

How to start selling this week, honestly. Step one: sell what you already own first. Pick five items in your home you no longer use, photograph them in daylight, and list them on Facebook Marketplace or eBay. Step two: price from sold listings, not listed prices — on eBay, filter for sold items. A jacket listed at $60 that never sold is not worth $60; a jacket that sold three times at $25 is. Step three: pick one lane and learn it — thrift flipping, handmade goods, and print-on-demand are three different businesses, and trying all three at once usually means doing none of them well. Step four: reinvest the first sales slowly. Selling items you already own can bring a first sale within days; building a repeatable selling business usually takes one to three months.

The Tax Math Nobody Mentions

There is a second gap the lists skip: gross versus what you keep. Side income is taxable even if no form arrives. If your net self-employment earnings hit $400 in a year, the IRS expects a return and self-employment tax of 15.3% applies.

The form rules changed in 2025, and most guides still get them wrong. The IRS 1099-K reporting threshold is more than $20,000 in payments and more than 200 transactions in the calendar year — reinstated by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025), which replaced the planned phase-in to lower thresholds. That means a payment app or online marketplace only has to file a 1099-K for you when you pass both bars. Income below that threshold is still taxable, whether or not you receive a form — the threshold controls reporting, not taxability. A related change worth one line: the same law raised the 1099-NEC / 1099-MISC reporting threshold from $600 to $2,000 for payments made starting in 2026. Do not confuse the two forms: 1099-K covers payment apps and marketplaces; 1099-NEC covers direct payments from clients.

Many guides suggest setting aside 25% to 30% of side-hustle income for quarterly estimated taxes. So an $810 gross month is closer to $570 to $600 in your pocket after tax and typical platform fees. Quarterly estimated payments are generally required if you expect to owe $1,000 or more not covered by withholding.

The Cost Side: Burnout Is Measurable

Every side-hustle guru shows you the extra $500 a month. Almost nobody shows you the ledger's other side: the lost sleep, the wrecked focus at your main job, the weekends that disappeared. A poll published in October 2026 puts hard numbers on it — and they are worth reading before you take on another shift.

The PEMCO Insurance Northwest Poll (October 2026, residents of Washington and Oregon) found:

  • Half (50%) currently have a side hustle or had one in the past. Another 33% have considered one. And 90% say extra work is simply necessary for some people to get by in their region.
  • Of those with a hustle, 54% say it increased their stress or fatigue. Sleep was the most commonly affected area — 54% report their sleep disrupted — followed by focus/concentration (40%) and mental health (39%).
  • 78% see their side hustle as a financial necessity, not a passion project. The "do what you love" story is the exception, not the rule.
  • The most common hustles: gig/app-based work like delivery and rideshare (30%), creative work (22%), freelance or contract work (20%), selling or reselling goods (18%), and small businesses (10%).
  • Half invest more than 10 hours a week on top of their main job; about a quarter of those spend more than 15 hours.

The takeaway is not "side hustles are bad." It is that the real question is not how much does it pay — it is what does it cost. Before you commit, run three numbers:

  1. True hourly pay. Take the gross payout, subtract real expenses (gas, car wear, delivery bags, platform fees), and divide by all the hours — including waiting, driving to the hotspot, and admin. A $100 delivery day that took 8 hours and burned $30 in gas is $8.75/hour, not $12.50.
  2. The sleep tax. If the hustle eats into your sleep, price it: one bad night makes your main job worse. If your main job pays $25/hour and the hustle makes you 10% slower for a day, that's $20 of hidden cost against an $80 hustle evening.
  3. The exit test. Give every new hustle a 60-day review date. If after 60 days the true hourly pay is under what you'd accept for overtime at your main job — or your sleep and focus still haven't recovered — cut the hours or quit. Sunk cost is not a reason to keep going.

How to hustle without burning out: cap it at 10 hours a week unless you have run the math and it still wins; pick low-drain hustles — freelance work that uses skills you already have (writing, design, bookkeeping) beats delivery work that trades your body and your sleep for variable pay; batch it — two focused 4-hour blocks beat seven exhausted 1-hour scraps after work; and protect sleep first, because a hustle that pays $200 a month but wrecks your health is a loan from your body at terrible interest.

How to Actually Get to $1,000

The honest path is unglamorous. Pick one skill-based hustle — freelance writing, tutoring, bookkeeping, or an AI service for local businesses — and give it 90 days. Skilled freelancers commonly reach $1,500 to $4,000 a month at 10 hours a week once they have clients, but most take 2 to 8 weeks to see their first income, and replacement-level income usually takes 9 to 18 months. Track your hours and your hourly rate, not just your monthly total: a hustle paying $15 an hour needs 67 hours a month to hit $1,000, while one paying $50 an hour needs 20.

Set your first target at $200 a month — the median. Beat the median first, then chase the average, then the $1,000. That is the order the numbers actually work in.

How much of my side hustle income do I actually keep?

Do side hustles cause burnout?

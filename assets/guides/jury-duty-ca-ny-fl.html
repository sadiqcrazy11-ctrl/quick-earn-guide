Jury Duty Pay by State (2026): California, New York & Florida

Quick Answer: Jury duty pay by state is set by state law, and these three states could hardly differ more. California pays nothing on day one, then $15/day from the second day, plus $0.34/mile. New York pays $72/day — the “$40 a day” figure all over the internet is the old rate; the current Judiciary Law §521 says $72. Florida pays $15/day for the first 3 days, then $30/day — but only if you are not regularly employed or your employer stops your wages; if your pay continues, the state pays nothing for those first 3 days. The federal baseline for comparison is $50/day. In all three states, your employer cannot legally fire you for serving. Every figure below comes from the statute or court source itself — each state section links it.

California: $0 the first day, $15 after — plus mileage

California's rule is in Code of Civil Procedure §215: a juror is paid $15 “for each day's attendance as a juror after the first day” — the first day of service pays nothing from the court. From that second day on, the state also reimburses mileage at $0.34 per mile for travel to and from court, and public-transit costs up to $12 a day. One exception printed in the same statute: if you work for a public entity and your regular compensation continues during service, you do not also collect the juror fee. On the job side, Labor Code §230(a) says an employer “shall not discharge or in any manner discriminate against” an employee for taking time off to serve, as long as the employee gives reasonable notice — but no California law requires your employer to keep paying your wages while you serve. Some do, as a policy; that is between you and your handbook.

New York: it's $72 a day now — the $40 figure is outdated

Search New York jury pay and most pages will tell you $40 a day. That was the rate in Judiciary Law §521 — but the current statute text reads “seventy-two dollars per day for each and every day of physical attendance,” and §521-a adds $6 per day for service beyond 30 days. If your daily wages are less than $72, the state allowance tops you up to $72; if your wages are not withheld at all, no per-diem is paid for those days. New York is also the only state of the three that puts a wage duty on employers: under Judiciary Law §519, an employer with more than 10 employees “shall not withhold the first seventy-two dollars” of your daily wages during the first three days of jury service — and the same section bars any employer from discharging or penalizing you for serving, if you notified them beforehand. One honest gap: §521 does not set a per-mile travel rate, so mileage varies — check your summons or your county's jury office rather than trusting any number a blog gives you.

Florida: $15, then $30 — and only if your wages stop

Florida Statutes §40.24 has the most conditional rule of the three. For the first 3 days, the state pays $15/day only if you are not regularly employed, or your employer does not continue your wages during service. If you are regularly employed and your pay continues, the state pays nothing for those days — your salary is your jury pay. From the fourth day onward, every juror gets $30/day. And Florida is blunt about travel: §40.24(5) says jurors “are not entitled to additional reimbursement” for travel or other out-of-pocket expenses — there is no mileage. Your job itself is protected by F.S. §40.271: no employer may fire you “because of the nature or length” of your jury service, and threatening to do so can be treated as contempt of court. But like California, Florida does not require your employer to pay your wages while you serve.

Honest disclaimer: This guide is general information about juror pay, not legal advice. Your exact pay is whatever your summons and your court's jury office say — counties administer these rules, and details can differ by court and by whether you land in state or federal court. If your employer retaliates over jury service, that is a legal problem worth taking to an employment lawyer or your state labor agency.

FAQ

Q: How much does jury duty pay in California, New York, and Florida?
A: California pays $0 on the first day and $15/day from the second day, plus $0.34/mile (Code of Civil Procedure §215). New York pays $72/day (Judiciary Law §521), plus $6/day after 30 days. Florida pays $15/day for the first 3 days and $30/day from day 4 — but the first 3 days pay nothing from the state if you are regularly employed and your wages continue (F.S. §40.24). Federal courts pay $50/day in every state.

Q: Is New York jury duty pay really $40 a day?
A: Not anymore. $40 was the old figure in Judiciary Law §521, and it is still repeated all over the internet. The current statute sets the juror allowance at $72 per day. Employers with more than 10 employees must cover at least the first $72 of your daily wages for the first 3 days of service (Judiciary Law §519).

Q: Can my employer fire me for jury duty in California, New York, or Florida?
A: No. All three states protect your job: California Labor Code §230 bars discharging or discriminating against an employee for jury service (with reasonable notice given); New York Judiciary Law §519 bars discharge or penalty; Florida Statutes §40.271 bars dismissal because of the nature or length of service. Federal law protects federal-court jurors the same way.

Q: Does my employer have to pay my wages while I serve on a jury?
A: In New York, partially: employers with more than 10 employees must pay at least the first $72 of your daily wages for the first 3 days (Judiciary Law §519). In California and Florida, no — neither state requires private employers to pay wages during jury service, though many employers do voluntarily as a policy. Check your employee handbook before you serve.

Quick Answer: California substitute teachers are paid per day, and each district sets its own rate — there is no statewide sub salary. In the largest districts for 2026-27, the standard day-to-day rate runs from $200/day (Corona-Norco) to $282.93/day (Los Angeles USD), and long-term assignments pay more: Sacramento City USD's schedule pays $400.72/day after the first day of service. One thing that surprises people coming from Texas: California's 30-Day Substitute Teaching Permit requires a bachelor's degree, so there is no “non-degreed” classroom tier in most districts — the table below shows how each district actually splits its pay. California has nearly 1,000 school districts, and substitute pay is set district by district, board vote by board vote. That is why the numbers floating around job boards are a mess of old schedules and guesswork. So, as with our Texas guide, we went to the source: each district's own substitute pay schedule or HR page. Here is what the biggest districts in California actually pay — and, just as honestly, the three big districts whose current rate we could not verify from an official page, marked “Check district site” rather than filled in with a guess.

How to become a substitute teacher in California

Unlike Texas, California subs are credentialed by the state. The standard route is the 30-Day Substitute Teaching Permit, issued by the California Commission on Teacher Credentialing (CTC). Per the CTC's own leaflet (CL-505p):

  1. Hold a bachelor's degree. You need official transcripts showing a baccalaureate or higher degree from a regionally accredited college or university. There is no way around this for the 30-Day Permit — it is the core requirement.
  2. The CBEST question — mostly settled. You used to have to pass the CBEST or otherwise prove basic skills on top of the degree. Since June 29, 2024, the bachelor's degree itself satisfies both the degree requirement and the Basic Skills Requirement for this permit, per the CTC leaflet. (The CBEST still matters for full teaching credentials — just not for the 30-Day Permit.)
  3. Apply through CTC Online and get fingerprinted. Submit the application online, complete the fingerprint (Live Scan) process if you have not already done it for the Commission, and pay the application processing fee.
  4. Know the permit's limits. The permit is valid for one year and renewable. It authorizes day-to-day subbing in any classroom, but no more than 30 days for any one teacher in a school year (20 days in special education). Your employing district must keep a Statement of Need on file.
  5. Apply to districts and pick your days. With the permit in hand, apply on each district's HR page (or through an agency — see below), complete its orientation, and accept assignments through its absence system.

Direct hire vs. agency: Swing Education, Kelly Education and ESS

California subs reach the classroom two ways:

  • Direct hire. The district employs you and pays you the district's published rate from the table above. Most of the large districts listed here hire this way.
  • Staffing agency or platform. Some districts and many charter schools staff subs through an agency. Swing Education runs a marketplace-style platform that is especially common in California; Kelly Education and ESS are the other big names in school staffing. One application can cover several schools.

The trade-off is the same as in Texas: direct hire means the full published rate and a direct line to the district's HR office; agency work can mean faster onboarding and more schools to choose from, but the agency sets your pay, and it may not match the district's own rate for the same classroom. You need the same state permit and fingerprint clearance either way. Get the exact daily rate in writing before your first assignment — if they won't give it, that tells you what you need to know.

Honest disclaimer: Substitute pay rates change every school year, and districts can adjust them mid-year. Every figure in this guide was taken from the district's own published schedule and checked in October 2026, but treat the table as a starting point — verify the current rate on your district's site before you plan income around it.

FAQ

Q: How much do substitute teachers get paid per day in California?
A: Each district sets its own rate. In the largest districts for 2026-27, the standard day-to-day rate runs from $200/day (Corona-Norco USD) to $282.93/day (Los Angeles USD), with San Diego at $213.54, Long Beach at $217.68, Fresno at $211.15 and Elk Grove at $210. Long-term and specialized assignments pay more — Sacramento City USD pays $400.72/day after the first day of service, and San Francisco's FAQ lists $325.82/day. Rates change yearly — check your district's own schedule.

Q: Do you need a degree to be a substitute teacher in California?
A: Yes, for the standard route. The California Commission on Teacher Credentialing's 30-Day Substitute Teaching Permit requires a bachelor's degree (or higher) from a regionally accredited college or university, shown on official transcripts. That is why most California districts publish one classroom rate instead of separate degreed and non-degreed tiers.

Q: Do I still need to pass the CBEST to substitute teach in California?
A: Not for the 30-Day Substitute Teaching Permit. Since June 29, 2024, the bachelor's degree itself satisfies both the degree requirement and the Basic Skills Requirement for this permit, according to the CTC's leaflet CL-505p. The CBEST still applies to other credentials, such as full teaching credentials.

Q: Is it better to work directly for a district or through an agency like Swing Education?
A: Direct hire pays the district's full published rate and puts you on the district's own sub list. Agencies and platforms like Swing Education, Kelly Education and ESS can onboard you faster and cover several schools with one application, but the agency sets your pay and it may be lower than the district's rate. Either way you need the same state permit and fingerprint clearance — and you should get the daily rate in writing before your first assignment.

How to Make Money on YouTube (Honest Starter Guide)

By Sadiq Khan · Published October 2026 · 11 min read · Updated on November 21, 2026

Share:WhatsAppTelegramX

Quick Answer: You make money on YouTube by joining the YouTube Partner Program (YPP) — currently 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 valid public watch hours in 12 months, or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days — and then earning a share of ad revenue: 55% of net revenue from ads on long-form videos and 45% of the revenue allocated to you from the Shorts Creator Pool. AI-made videos can be monetized too, but only if each video is original and genuinely useful — mass-produced template videos, slideshows with no commentary, and AI personas giving money or health advice are blocked as "inauthentic content." Realistic beginners should plan on 4–6 months of consistent, original uploads before reaching monetization, and longer before the income is meaningful.

YouTube is one of the most proven ways to earn online — and one of the slowest to start. Both things are true. The creators who make real money treated it like a part-time job for months before the first check arrived. This guide covers the real requirements, the real pay, the AI rules that now decide whether a channel gets monetized, and the first steps that actually work.

The requirements: YouTube Partner Program

You cannot earn ad revenue until YouTube accepts you into the Partner Program. The current thresholds are:

  • Path A (long videos): 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 valid public watch hours in the last 12 months.
  • Path B (Shorts): 1,000 subscribers and 10 million valid Shorts views in the last 90 days.
  • You also need an AdSense account, two-step verification on your Google account, and no active Community Guidelines strikes.

Upcoming change for new applicants: From February 1, 2027, new applicants to the YouTube Partner Program ad and Premium revenue-sharing program will need 1,000 subscribers plus either 8,000 qualified public watch hours in the previous 365 days or 20 million qualified Shorts views in the previous 90 days — up from 4,000 watch hours and 10 million Shorts views. The 1,000-subscriber requirement is unchanged. Creators already in the program are not removed solely for falling below the new entry thresholds. That change matters for timing: if you are close to the current bar, reaching it before February 1, 2027 means applying under the lower threshold. Existing partners must also maintain 10 million Shorts views in 90 days to keep earning from the Shorts pool, a separate maintenance rule worth knowing before you build a Shorts-only channel.

How the pay actually works

For eligible creators who accept the relevant monetization terms, YouTube pays 55% of net revenue from ads shown on public videos on the Watch Page. Shorts Feed monetization works differently: creators receive 45% of the revenue allocated to them from the Shorts Creator Pool. These percentages are not a guarantee of earnings, and actual income varies by format, audience, advertiser demand, and other factors. Check YouTube's official monetization documentation for current terms.

What RPM means: RPM is creator earnings per 1,000 views — the amount the creator actually receives. It is not CPM, which is what advertisers pay before YouTube's share. The figures in the table below are illustrative planning estimates only: they are not verified industry averages and they are not guaranteed payouts.

Estimated revenue = monetized views ÷ 1,000 × assumed RPM. Actual results may differ.

A worked example makes the gap concrete. A tech how-to channel with 100,000 monetized views in a month at an illustrative $6 RPM would estimate $600 before tax — while an entertainment channel with the same 100,000 views at $2 RPM would estimate $200. Same views, three times the difference, decided by niche and audience. And views are not all monetized: some viewers use ad blockers, some watch without ads, and Shorts views pay at a far lower rate per view than long-form views. That is why channels that survive stack other income — affiliate links, sponsorships, and digital products — on top of ads, so one policy change or one slow month cannot wipe them out.

Can AI videos be monetized? The real rules

Yes — using AI does not disqualify you from YouTube monetization. What YouTube blocks is "inauthentic content": mass-produced template videos, image slideshows with no real commentary, and AI personas giving money or health advice. AI visuals are fine when the story, characters, and angle are originally yours.

In July 2025, YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" policy to "inauthentic content" and clarified that it covers mass-produced AI videos. The rule never names a tool. It asks one question about the finished video: is it original and does it give viewers something?

What YouTube's policy explicitly allows:

  • Using AI to visualize a unique character and story you invented
  • A series following the same characters, as long as each episode has its own distinct storyline
  • AI voices for narration, AI-assisted editing, AI-generated backgrounds for your content

What YouTube's policy blocks from monetization:

  • AI content made with generic or unoriginal templates — the same prompt and assets across dozens of uploads
  • Image slideshows or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative, commentary, or educational value
  • Videos stitched from unrelated AI clips just to shock viewers
  • AI "podcast hosts" giving financial advice or an AI "doctor" giving health advice

The practical test is simple: would a real person sit through it and benefit? Write your own script or angle for every video, add narration or commentary that adds something (not just reading the slideshow), and make each upload visibly different from the last. AI-assisted content — a ChatGPT draft, an AI voice, AI visuals — can all be monetized if the result has meaningful human editorial involvement. One original video per day beats ten template uploads. The channels that fail are the ones that post near-identical template videos daily and wonder why earnings never start. AI saves production time; it does not skip the audience-building.

Step by step: tick the disclosure checkbox

YouTube requires disclosure for realistic synthetic content: an AI face, a synthetic voice presenting as a real person, or an AI depiction of an event that did not happen. It is one checkbox at upload in YouTube Studio — the "altered or synthetic content" disclosure. Animation, fantasy visuals, and AI-assisted scripts or captions do not need it, and cloning your own voice for voiceovers does not need it either. You only tick the box for realistic content that could mislead viewers, such as a real person appearing to say something they did not.

YouTube says disclosing does not hurt your reach or your ability to earn money. Skipping the disclosure when it is required is the mistake: YouTube says creators who consistently choose not to disclose may be subject to manual application of a label or penalties from YouTube, including removal of content or suspension from the YouTube Partner Program. So tick the box whenever it applies, and do not treat disclosure as something to hide — it is a routine part of uploading realistic AI footage.

On TikTok the equivalent rule is the platform's AI label for realistic AI content. TikTok's Creator Rewards Program pays only on original videos at least one minute long, and to qualify you generally need to be 18 or older, have at least 10,000 followers, and have 100,000 video views in the last 30 days. The question on both platforms is never "is it AI?" — it is "is it original?"

The honest timeline

There is no reliable universal timeline for reaching monetization. Some channels qualify within months, others take years, and some never qualify. Progress depends on your topic, publishing consistency, audience response, watch time, and meeting YouTube's monetization policies. Treat any timeline — including any planning scenario in this guide — as a planning scenario, not a prediction.

Creators running original AI-assisted channels report hitting the Partner Program in 4–6 months of real effort, then earning an estimated $200–$1,000 a month in ad revenue at small scale — growing with the catalog. That range is a reported early-stage estimate, not a promise: your niche, audience location, and upload consistency decide where you land, and many channels earn far less in their first monetized months. Faceless channels that upload 2–3 original videos per week for 4–6 months are the ones that cross the line. The "AI video made me $10,000 in a week" screenshots are personal results or paid promotions, not guarantees. Treat the first months as building an asset, not cashing a check.

Your first steps

  1. Pick one narrow topic you can make 50 videos about — a specific hobby, skill, or problem you understand.
  2. Study 5 channels in that niche. Note which of their videos got far more views than their subscriber count. Make your own version of those ideas — never copies.
  3. Post consistently: one long video a week, or 3–5 Shorts a week if you go the Shorts route. Consistency beats expensive gear.
  4. Use what you own. A modern phone, free editing software (CapCut, DaVinci Resolve), and natural light are enough to start.
  5. Review your analytics every month. Double down on the topics and titles viewers actually click and watch.
  6. Apply to the Partner Program the moment you cross a threshold, and set up AdSense correctly with your legal name and US tax information.

Shorts or long-form? Use both, for different jobs

Long-form pays higher RPM and builds watch hours, but Shorts grow subscribers faster. Most successful faceless channels use Shorts for discovery and long-form for revenue — the same content idea often becomes both. A Short that performs well tells you the topic is worth a full video; a long video that holds viewers tells you the topic deserves more Shorts. Do not build a channel on one format alone if you can avoid it: a Shorts-only channel earns at the lower Shorts rate and must keep clearing the 10-million-view maintenance bar, while a long-form-only channel grows subscribers more slowly. The split is a strategy choice, not a rule — but the channels that last usually run both.

Common mistakes

  • Repeating one template. Same assets, same structure, swapped words, across dozens of uploads. That reads as mass-produced and puts the whole channel's monetization at risk.
  • Skipping disclosure on realistic AI footage. It takes seconds at upload; skipping it when required can lead to YouTube adding the label itself, removal of the video, or suspension from the Partner Program.
  • Chasing the threshold with low-effort volume. Ten template uploads a day will not build the watch time or the audience that the Partner Program actually requires. Originality is the gate, not upload count.
  • Pricing your expectations on someone else's RPM. A finance channel's $15 RPM says nothing about what your entertainment channel will earn. Use your own Analytics once you are monetized.
  • Starting with expensive gear. A phone, free editing software, and natural light are enough. Gear does not fix a weak idea.
  • Quitting at month two. Most channels earn $0 for months. The creators who cross the line are the ones who kept uploading original videos while nothing was paying yet.

Honest bottom line: YouTube rewards patience. If you need money this month, start with freelancing and build your channel on the side. If you can give it a year, a channel becomes an asset that keeps earning from old videos.

FAQs

Will YouTube demonetize my channel just because I use AI voices or AI images?

No. YouTube's policies judge whether each video is original and varied, not which tool made the voice or images. Disclosure alone will not affect your eligibility to earn.

Does the AI disclosure checkbox hurt my views?

No — YouTube says disclosing synthetic content does not hurt reach or earnings. Failing to disclose when required is what can lead to penalties, including removal of the video or suspension from the Partner Program.

Is it better to do long-form or Shorts with AI?

Long-form pays higher RPM and builds watch hours, but Shorts grow subscribers faster. Most successful faceless channels use Shorts for discovery and long-form for revenue.

Do I need to disclose if I clone my own voice for voiceovers?

No. YouTube's no-disclosure list includes cloning your own voice. You only tick the "altered or synthetic content" box for realistic content that could mislead viewers.

What is the #1 mistake that kills AI channel monetization?

Repeating one template — same assets, same structure, swapped words — across dozens of uploads. That reads as mass-produced and puts the whole channel at risk.

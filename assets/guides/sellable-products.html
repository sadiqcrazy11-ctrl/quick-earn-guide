Best Products to Sell Online: What Actually Sells (Prices, Costs & Free Tools)

By Sadiq Khan · Published October 2026 · 15 min read · Updated on November 21, 2026

Share:WhatsAppTelegramX

Quick Answer: The best products to sell online — the cheapest real ones — are digital: planners and printables on Etsy (start: a $0.20 listing fee), templates on Gumroad (start: $0), ebooks on Amazon KDP (start: $0), and print-on-demand shirts and mugs (start: $0 — you keep a $3–$10 margin per sale). Set honest expectations: most new sellers earn $0–$50 a month in their first months, and the free tools listed below are enough to start. Pay for tools only after sales cover them. Before you list anything, check what is actually rising — in late 2026 that meant Loop-style earplugs, lash clusters, and padel rackets — and verify the trend yourself before spending money.

Products beat services in one way: you make them once and they can sell while you sleep. They lose in another: nobody has to buy. These are the six product types beginners actually sell — what each one is, what it costs to start, what buyers pay, and what sellers honestly keep.

The screenshots lie by omission: For every seller posting a $10,000 month, thousands of shops with similar products made $0 — millions of sellers compete for the same Etsy and Google searches. Most new shops earn $0–$50 a month in their first months, and many never make a first sale at all. The sellers who last treat it as a slow catalog business: 20–50 useful listings, clear photos, one specific niche. Read the numbers below as the normal case, not the highlight reel.

Six products that actually sell

Planners & printables (Etsy)

PDF planners, calendars, habit trackers, and printable wall art. Make it once in free Canva, sell the same file forever. The catch: thousands of near-identical listings — a specific buyer (teachers, new parents, wedding planners) beats a generic one every time.

Start: $0–$4. Price: $5–$30. Where: Etsy, Gumroad.

Templates: Notion, Canva & spreadsheets

Resume templates, social-media kits, budget spreadsheets, Notion dashboards. Buyers pay to skip the setup work. Sell on Etsy or Gumroad, and bundle several templates together to raise the price buyers accept.

Start: $0. Price: $9–$49. Where: Gumroad, Etsy.

Ebooks & short guides (Amazon KDP)

A 30–60 page how-to on one narrow problem. Publishing on KDP is free; the royalty is 70% on ebooks priced $2.99–$9.99 and 35% outside that band. Most self-published ebooks sell a handful of copies — the income comes from a catalog, not one book.

Start: $0. Price: $2.99–$19.99. Where: Amazon KDP, Gumroad.

AI art prints & clipart (Etsy)

AI-generated wall art, clipart packs, and seamless patterns sold as instant downloads. Etsy's rule: you must disclose in the listing that the design was AI-created. This niche saturated fast — specific subjects (nursery art, one dog breed, one hobby) outsell generic sunsets.

Start: $0–$10. Price: $8–$40. Where: Etsy.

Prompt packs & AI resource bundles

Curated prompt collections for ChatGPT or image tools, sold as a PDF or Notion file. Cheap to make and hard to defend — anyone can copy a prompt, so the real value is curation, worked examples, and updates you keep adding. Note: Etsy has banned selling AI prompt bundles since 2024 — sell the finished artwork or product the prompt creates, not the prompt itself, and check the platform's current rules before listing.

Start: $0. Price: $7–$39. Where: Gumroad.

Print-on-demand (Printful, Redbubble)

Your design on tees, mugs, and posters that a partner prints and ships only when someone orders. Zero inventory: you keep the gap between the base cost and your price, usually $3–$10 per item. Many designs, one niche audience — that is the whole game.

Start: $0. Margin: $3–$10 per item. Where: Printful, Redbubble, Printify.

Etsy's standard US fees work out to about 9.5% + $0.45 per order: a $0.20 listing fee, a 6.5% transaction fee, and 3% + $0.25 payment processing. On a $10 planner sale, roughly $1.10 goes to Etsy before any ad costs. A $3 printable leaves very little room after that, so the typical price in your niche has to cover fees and your time, or the niche fails no matter how trendy it looks. Fees and royalties change — every platform above has adjusted them in the last few years. Check the current fee page before you price anything, and price so the product still works after the cut.

A worked example: you sell a $12 planner on Etsy. Etsy takes $0.20 (listing) + $0.78 (6.5% transaction) + $0.61 (3% + $0.25 processing) — about $1.59 total, leaving about $10.41 before tax and before any ad spend. Sell ten of them in a month and that is about $104 before tax. Sell none — the normal first month — and the cost is $0.20 per listing. That asymmetry is the whole business: the downside is tiny, the upside is slow, and the middle is where most sellers live.

What is trending: three risers, and how to check any trend yourself

Three products were genuinely rising in search demand in late 2026, according to Exploding Topics trend data checked October 2026 — but rising searches are NOT the same as easy money. Most people who chase trends lose. Verify the trend yourself, enter early or niche down, and test with print-on-demand or a tiny batch before spending real money.

Loop-style earplugs — about 673,000 searches a month, up 82%. Soft loop-shaped earplugs that lower noise instead of blocking it completely — for sleep, focus, concerts, and noisy homes. They are rising because sleep and focus are now mainstream wellness topics, and one brand (Loop) made earplugs look like jewelry instead of industrial foam. Selling angle: niche down — "earplugs for light sleepers with snoring partners" beats a generic listing every time.

Lash clusters — about 246,000 searches a month, up 250%. DIY lash-extension kits: small clusters you apply under your own lashes at home for a salon look — without the salon price ($100+ per set) or the appointment. TikTok tutorials and that cost gap are driving the rise. Selling angle: "beginner lash kits with tutorial" — bundle the clusters, glue, and tweezers with a step-by-step card for first-timers.

Padel rackets — about 301,000 searches a month, up 146%. Padel — a racket sport mixing tennis and squash, played in doubles on an enclosed court — is booming outside its home markets of Spain and Latin America, with new clubs opening across the US, UK, and Middle East. Every new player needs a first racket. Selling angle: "beginner padel sets" — a racket, balls, and a bag bundled for someone's first month of play.

Search volumes and growth figures move every month — re-check them before you spend anything. Here is the free method to check any trend yourself:

  1. Google Trends — use the 5-year view. Not just the last 30 days. A spike that dies in weeks is a fad; a line that climbs steadily for a year is a trend.
  2. Exploding Topics and TikTok search. See whether real people are posting about it — reviews, unboxings, complaints — or whether it is only ads.
  3. Amazon and Etsy search. Count how many sellers already list it. A handful means there is room; 500 sellers means you are late.
  4. Check the trend's "why." A real need — sleep, a beauty routine, a new sport — lasts. A single celebrity mention usually does not.

Why most trend-chasers lose money: they buy inventory AFTER the peak, when search interest is already falling. They compete with the factories themselves on price, so the margin vanishes. They pay ad costs that eat what is left of the margin, because late entrants have to shout louder. And they end up holding stock nobody wants, discounted to zero. The winners usually sell the "picks and shovels" instead — accessories, guides, and bundles around the trend — or enter a narrow sub-niche the factories ignore.

Low-risk ways to test: test before you stock, with print-on-demand or a small dropship test first — real orders with near-zero inventory risk. List where buyers already are — Etsy and Amazon before your own store, because their built-in search traffic beats an empty website. Run the margin math first, before you list, not after. Set a hard test budget: $50–$100. If the product does not sell inside that budget, kill it and move on. The budget is the tuition — do not let it become rent money.

Sell other people's stuff: the resale concierge model

You do not have to own inventory to sell products. A resale concierge sells other people's stuff for them — you photograph, list, and ship their items on eBay, Poshmark, or Facebook Marketplace, and keep a commission of roughly 25 to 50 percent of each sale. You buy no inventory, so your startup cost is close to zero. Your first client is usually someone you already know with a closet, garage, or storage unit full of things they will never list themselves.

How the money works: a client hands you items, you research the price, photograph, list, answer buyer questions, pack, and ship. When the item sells, you take your cut and pay the owner the rest. The standard splits look like this: an independent concierge typically takes 40 to 50 percent of the sale price for a full-service job — you do everything, including shipping. Some sellers use a net split instead: platform fees and shipping come off the top first, then you split what is left. eBay's official consignment partners pay 60 percent to the owner on items under $300, rising to 80 percent on items over $5,000 — but that is their program, not yours, and it is aimed at luxury items.

The honest math: a $100 sale at a 50 percent split is $50 to you before fees. Ten items a month at that level is $500 — real money, but it is ten full listing-and-shipping jobs, not passive income.

What sells best: stick to items worth your time. A $15 item that takes 30 minutes to list, photograph, and ship earns you about $7.50 — a bad deal. Good concierge items are designer clothes and handbags, collectibles, working electronics, brand-name sneakers, and quality furniture that sells locally. Before you accept anything, check sold listings on eBay or Poshmark to confirm the price, and be honest with the client when an item is worth less than they think.

Getting your first client: your first three clients are almost always people you know — a neighbor clearing a closet, a relative downsizing, a coworker moving house. Offer to sell five items as a trial, with a simple written agreement that states your commission, who pays shipping and platform fees, and when the owner gets paid — most experienced consignment sellers pay out 14 to 30 days after delivery. One good trial turns into referrals, because everyone knows someone with a full garage. Set up a simple tracking system from day one: give every item a code — client initials, item number, date — so nothing gets mixed up, and photograph items the moment they arrive. The biggest rookie mistake is taking in a pile of unlabeled stuff and then guessing whose it was.

The honest catch: items that do not sell still take up space in your home. Returns happen, and you need a written rule for who absorbs them. Some items need permission to sell — estate pieces, branded goods with authenticity questions — and you should never list something you cannot verify. Platform fees come out of the sale before anyone gets paid, so quote your client the net number, not the headline price. Rules vary by state and city, and some places require a resale or secondhand dealer license, especially for jewelry or high-value goods. Always use a written consignment agreement that states your commission, fees, payout timing, and what happens to unsold items — never take an item without one.

One seasonal note worth keeping: printables still sell 5 days out from a holiday. In the final days before Halloween, for example, printable party games, coloring packs, scavenger hunts, and invitation templates sold at $3–$12 because buyers download them in seconds while physical orders can no longer arrive in time — small, fast sales, not a windfall, with the window closing on the holiday itself. The lesson generalizes: for any holiday, list digital downloads 6–8 weeks early, and keep a few instant-download items live for the last-minute buyers who need a file tonight, not a package next week.

Discounts & free tools: start for $0

Every tool a beginner product seller actually needs has a free tier that is enough to launch. These are the real ones — no affiliate links in this section.

  • Canva Free. Design planners, ebook covers, and product mockups at $0 — the free tier is enough for your first products. Canva Pro (about $15 a month) adds premium elements and resizing you do not need on day one.
  • ChatGPT & Claude free tiers. Draft ebook chapters, listing titles, and product descriptions for $0. Edit everything before it ships — buyers can spot raw AI output, and marketplaces penalize listings that read like it.
  • Google Docs, Sheets & Drive (free account). Write the ebook, build the spreadsheet template, track sales — free with 15 GB of storage.
  • Gumroad. No monthly fee and no listing fee — you pay 10% + $0.50 only when a sale happens.
  • Amazon KDP. Publishing is free — Amazon takes its royalty share out of sales, never upfront. The same logic applies to Printful and Redbubble accounts: $0 to open, costs come out of each order.
  • CapCut (free). Product teaser videos and listing clips for Etsy and social posts, without an editor subscription. A 20-second video of your planner in use outsells a static cover photo.
Honest bottom line: Your first 20 listings can cost under $5 total — a few Etsy listing fees and $0 everywhere else. Spend your first month on products and photos, not subscriptions. Upgrade a tool only when a paying customer makes the free tier feel small, and be suspicious of any "discount" that asks for your card before you have sold anything.

Common mistakes

  • Listing one perfect product. Sellers who earn steadily run 20–50 listings in one clear niche, not one perfect product.
  • Pricing without fees. A $10 Etsy sale keeps about $8.90 after fees, before tax and ads. Price so the product still works after the cut.
  • Chasing a trend after the peak. Check Google Trends' 5-year chart: if searches peaked months ago and are falling, and marketplaces are crowded with discount sellers, you are late.
  • Copying a bestseller. Same photos, same title, same design — Etsy and buyers both punish copies. Make your own version of the idea.
  • Trusting a tool's sales estimate as fact. Estimates compare ideas; they do not forecast your income.
  • Skipping the trademark check. A trending phrase tied to a brand or character can get a listing removed.
  • Taking concierge items without a written agreement. Commission, fees, payout timing, and what happens to unsold items — in writing, before you take anything.

FAQs

What are the best digital products for a beginner to sell?

Templates and planners lead on profit because they cost almost nothing to make — a Canva template, Notion dashboard, or planner priced at $9–$49 costs time, not inventory. The honest asterisk: most individual listings sell nothing. Sellers who earn steadily run 20–50 listings in one clear niche, not one perfect product.

How much does it cost to start selling on Etsy?

$0.20 per listing (each lasts 4 months), plus a 6.5% transaction fee and payment processing when something sells — about 9.5% + $0.45 per order in total. A first batch of 10–20 products designed free in Canva costs $2–$4 in listing fees — under $20 all-in.

Is print-on-demand really free to start?

Yes — Printful, Printify, and Redbubble charge no signup or inventory fees; production and shipping are deducted from each order. The trade-off is margin: on a $25 t-shirt you typically keep $3–$10.

How much can you make as a resale concierge?

At a 40 to 50 percent commission, ten $100 sales a month is about $400 to $500 before platform fees and shipping. High-value items pay much more per job, but low-value items can eat your time for a few dollars each — so the real skill is choosing which items to accept.

Does a trending product guarantee sales?

No. Search interest means people are curious, not that they will buy from you; competition, price, and timing decide that.

Rideshare and Delivery Driving in 2026: The Honest Money Guide

By Sadiq Khan · Published November 19, 2026 · 3 min read · Updated on November 19, 2026

Share:WhatsAppTelegramX

Quick Answer: Driving pays real money — many drivers report roughly $15–$25 an hour before expenses in busy markets — but gas, wear, and taxes take a big cut. Track every mile: the IRS lets you deduct 76 cents per business mile for July–December 2026, often worth more than the gas you actually burned.

Driving for Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Instacart is the most flexible side hustle in America: you choose your hours, you need no interview, and you can start within days. It is also the hustle where beginners most overestimate their pay — because the app shows gross earnings, and the real number comes after gas, maintenance, depreciation, and taxes.

What the platforms pay

Pay varies by city, hour, and platform — the apps publish no flat hourly rate. Before expenses, many drivers report roughly $15–$25 an hour in busy markets and peak hours — late nights, lunch rushes, bad weather, and big events pay best; quiet afternoons pay worst. Delivery adds tips — a real part of income on DoorDash and Uber Eats. Instacart pays per batch plus tips, and fast shoppers earn more per hour. The only number that matters is your own: track a full week, subtract costs, and divide by hours actually worked, including waiting time.

The mileage deduction is the whole game

The IRS standard mileage rate for 2026 is 72.5 cents per mile for January–June and 76 cents per mile for July–December. That deduction covers gas, maintenance, insurance, and depreciation in one number — and for most drivers it beats their actual car costs. Drive 300 business miles in a week and the deduction is $228. Keep a mileage log from day one: the apps track some miles, but not the miles to the busy area or home after your last trip — and those count too.

The costs drivers forget

  • Your car wears out faster. 1,000 extra miles a month means tires, brakes, and oil changes arrive sooner, and resale value drops.
  • Insurance. Personal policies often exclude commercial driving; rideshare endorsements exist, and driving without one can void a claim.
  • Taxes are not withheld. You are an independent contractor. Set aside 20–30% of profit for taxes, or April will hurt.
  • Dead miles. Driving back empty after a drop-off earns nothing but costs gas and wear.

How to earn more per hour

  1. Drive the peaks, not the flats. Friday and Saturday nights, lunch rushes, rain, and events — that is where surge and tips live.
  2. Run two apps. Accept the better offer instead of waiting on one.
  3. Learn your city's map. Short trips in dense areas beat long highway runs once dead miles count.
  4. Keep the car clean and charged. Ratings drive tips; a dead phone ends your shift.

Bottom Line

Driving is honest, flexible money — roughly $400 a week gross for 20 hours at $20 an hour, minus gas and wear, before taxes. It is a good side hustle and a bad full-time plan for most people, because the car does half the work and the car does not get paid. Drive the peaks, log every mile, save for taxes.

FAQ

How much do Uber and DoorDash drivers actually make?
Before expenses, many drivers report roughly $15–$25 an hour in busy markets and peak hours. After gas, wear, and taxes, take-home is lower — track your own week, including waiting time, to know your real number.

What is the IRS mileage deduction for drivers in 2026?
72.5 cents per business mile for January–June 2026, and 76 cents per mile for July–December 2026. It covers gas, maintenance, insurance, and depreciation in one number — log every business mile, including driving to busy areas.

Is delivery driving worth it with gas prices?
It depends on your car and your market. Fuel-efficient cars and dense, short-trip areas work best; long highway trips with dead miles back often do not. The mileage deduction helps, but it does not make a bad market good.
